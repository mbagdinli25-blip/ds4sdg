Green to whom

Real greenness, marketed greenness and captured value around Istanbul's Nation's Gardens. Between 2021 and 2025 the Turkish state opened a wave of "Nation's Gardens" across Istanbul and presented them as green gifts to the city. This project asks what the satellites, the housing market and the official press releases actually show, and whether they agree.

// THE QUESTION

Did the green move with the price, or come apart from it?

Did Istanbul's Nation's Gardens bring real ecological change to the neighbourhoods around them, or did the market simply respond to the image of greenness, regardless of whether the green was real?

Announced by President Erdoğan in May 2018, the Nation's Gardens (Millet Bahçeleri) were introduced with the promise of bringing green space "to all areas of the city". TOKİ set out to build at least one in every Turkish province; in Istanbul a series of them opened on previously idle land.

Yet the same official documents that framed the gardens as ecological also described them as tools for "revitalising idle parts of the city and increasing their value", language that sits uneasily beside the distributive-justice commitments of the SDG 11.7 agenda. Where greening raises an area's appeal, it can raise prices and rents with it, displacing the very residents it was meant to serve: the dynamic the literature calls green gentrification.

This study separates the green from the image of the green. It tracks four things around each garden (vegetation, construction, the language of marketing, and prices) and asks whether they moved together, or came apart.

Ecological decoupling: when green marketing and rising prices diverge from the vegetation change that satellites actually record.
// WHAT WE ALREADY KNOW

Prices rise around new urban parks. Whether the parks are actually greener is rarely checked.

Green gentrification describes the socio-spatial inequalities that urban greening can produce. Adapting Smith's rent-gap theory, Anguelovski and colleagues identify a "green gap", the distance between an area's current and potential value that greening can unlock, often widest in lower-income neighbourhoods and on brownfield land.

These effects are not uniform across a city. In Barcelona, proximity to new green space strongly predicted demographic and income change in some districts while leaving more peripheral, stigmatised neighbourhoods untouched (Anguelovski et al., 2018): a heterogeneity that global models miss, and that this study's buffer-ring design is built to capture.

For Istanbul's Nation's Gardens specifically, Pala and Acar (2024) provide the pioneering account: comparing residential prices before and after opening for twelve gardens, they found evidence of green gentrification around ten of them.

But their study, like the field as a whole, leaves three questions open: did the gardens actually change the vegetation? how does green advertising language relate to prices? and are the price effects driven by the parks themselves or by city-wide trends? This project takes up exactly those gaps.

The design is deliberately descriptive. Matched comparison neighbourhoods and the staggered timing of the openings (2021–2024) provide causal support rather than proof. The ecological baseline is 2017, before any construction; the price window opens in 2021, when the data begin.
// FOUR GARDENS, OPENED IN DIFFERENT YEARS, IN DIFFERENT KINDS OF NEIGHBOURHOOD

The gardens, and how they are compared

The sample is built around staggered openings between 2021 and 2024, so that each garden offers a different length of "after", and so the timing of construction can be read against the timing of opening. The four span contrasting contexts, from Pendik's former quarry land in the east to Bağcılar's dense, already built-up west.

A fifth garden, Sultangazi-Cebeci, was set aside: it opened too recently to show a post-period, and sits on quarry-fill land with no residential neighbourhood around it for the value-capture mechanism to act on.

The comparison neighbourhoods. Each garden's treated neighbourhood is paired with comparison neighbourhoods chosen to resemble it before the garden opened, similar in price level and trajectory, so that any divergence afterwards can be read as the garden's effect rather than a city-wide tide. For Bağcılar, where no second comparable neighbourhood exists within the district, the matches come from adjacent Güngören.

// PRICES AND RENTS, TREATED VS COMPARISON

Who captured the value?

If the gardens captured value, prices around them should have outpaced comparable neighbourhoods. Mostly, they didn't.

Rebased to each garden's opening month, the treated neighbourhood's price and rent are set against the average of its matched comparisons. The result is selective, not systematic.

At Ümraniye (İnkılap) the treated neighbourhood pulls clearly ahead of its comparisons in both sale and rent: the one unambiguous case of value capture. At Pendik and Bağcılar the treated neighbourhood actually trails its comparisons; at Sultanbeyli the lead is slight. Value capture, where it happens, is the exception rather than the rule.

// THE SATELLITES VS THE BROCHURES

But did they actually green?

Now the satellites. They do not match the brochures.

Inside the gardens, not one gained tree cover, and two lost it. The starkest case is Sultanbeyli, where interior tree cover collapsed and built-up surface tripled; the vegetation index fell sharply in 2022 (before the garden even opened) and only partly recovered. Ümraniye lost roughly a fifth of its interior trees. Pendik and Bağcılar were never tree-covered to begin with: what greenness they added is grass, not canopy.

By 2025 the interiors are built-dominated: around half built at Pendik, three-fifths at Sultanbeyli, nearly two-thirds at Bağcılar.

Around the gardens, no ring gained vegetation anywhere. Where construction intensified, it did so only immediately next to the garden (the innermost 100 m at Pendik and Sultanbeyli) and faded to nothing beyond. Elsewhere the surroundings were already saturated.

// WHAT WAS PROMISED

The image of green

The gap between what was promised and what was measured is widest where it was sold hardest.

Sultanbeyli is the sharpest illustration. Official communications announced some 200,000 m² of new green space and 2,591 trees, presenting the garden as a source of "fresh air" and an "ecological agricultural garden". Yet the first saplings were planted only in late October (the "trees" are seedlings), and over the same site the satellites recorded tree cover falling steeply and built-up surface tripling.

// CONCLUSION
Across the four gardens, none produced a measurable gain in the vegetation of its surrounding neighbourhoods. The cases run from ecological decoupling (green in language and, sometimes, in price, but not on the ground) to null, where nothing around the garden changed at all.

The greenness the gardens delivered is, in land-cover terms, lawn and hard surface and image, more than canopy. Where value was captured, at Ümraniye, it was captured through the image of the garden rather than through any new green around it. The ecological promise and the financial gain did not arrive together, and neither arrived as ecological improvement for the neighbourhood.

On this evidence (descriptive, and drawn from four gardens), the benefits of this state-led greening did not reach the surrounding neighbourhoods as ecology. The green that moved the market was largely the green of the brochure.

Green to whom? Not, on this evidence, to the ecology of these neighbourhoods.

Descriptive design, N = 4 gardens. Matched comparison neighbourhoods and staggered openings provide causal support, not proof.
